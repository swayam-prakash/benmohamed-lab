Why our work matters

Herpes and coronaviruses hide in our tissues for life. Most people control them. We study why, and build vaccines that teach everyone else to do the same.

  • 530M+

    people worldwide live with genital herpes, with no therapeutic vaccine available.

  • 60M+

    Americans are affected by recurrent genital herpes alone.

  • $7.1M

    in new NIH awards to the lab in 2026 for ocular and genital herpes immunotherapies.

  • 30+ yrs

    of viral immunity and vaccine development, from the Pasteur Institute to UC Irvine.

Prevalence and award figures: UC Irvine School of Medicine, June 2026 and September 2026.

  1. 01

    The question

    Why do some infected people stay asymptomatic while others suffer recurrent, sometimes blinding disease or severe COVID-19? The answer lies in the quality and location of their T cells.

  2. 02

    The discovery

    We map B, CD4+ and CD8+ T cell epitopes that are selectively recognized by asymptomatic individuals, and the tissue-resident memory T cells that keep latent virus in check.

  3. 03

    The platform

    Epitope-based, multi-antigen vaccines delivered with mucosal chemokines and modern vectors, including mRNA lipid nanoparticles and adenovirus, in HLA transgenic models.

  4. 04

    The translation

    Three coronavirus patents from this laboratory are exclusively licensed to TechImmune, which is advancing T cell immunotherapies toward the clinic.

Research programs

Three programs, one idea: put protective immunity where the virus lives.

Program 01 · HSV-1

Recurrent ocular herpes

HSV-1 establishes lifelong latency in the trigeminal ganglia and is a leading infectious cause of blindness. Using an HLA transgenic model that reproduces reactivation, recurrent ocular disease, and the associated stress and behavioral changes, the lab showed that the latency-associated transcript (LAT) drives exhaustion of protective CD4+ and CD8+ tissue-resident memory T cells.

A new NIH award now tests whether an immunotherapy that selectively expands effector-memory and tissue-resident T cell subsets can reduce reactivation and recurrent disease.

  • LAT
  • TRM cells
  • CD57+ senescence
  • Gut-eye axis

Program 02 · HSV-2

Prime/Pull/Keep therapeutic vaccines

Instead of relying mainly on antibodies, this strategy first primes protective T cells, then pulls them into the tissues where HSV-2 hides and reactivates using mucosal chemokines such as CXCL11, and finally keeps them there for long-lasting immune surveillance.

  1. PrimeAsymptomatic epitope-based antigens expand virus-specific CD4+ and CD8+ T cells.
  2. PullChemokines recruit those cells into infected mucosal and neural tissues.
  3. KeepTissue-resident memory T cells persist on site to block recurrence.

Program 03 · Coronaviruses

Preemptive pan-coronavirus vaccines

Since early 2020 the team has identified B and T cell epitopes conserved between human and animal coronaviruses and preferentially recognized by asymptomatic COVID-19 patients. Combining those conserved non-spike antigens with spike protected mice and hamsters against both Delta and Omicron SARS-CoV-2.

The program also studies CXCL13/CXCR5 lung immunity in severe COVID-19 and persistent SARS-CoV-2 reservoirs in chronic Long COVID.

  • Conserved epitopes
  • Multi-antigen
  • Mucosal delivery
  • Long COVID

From the lab to the clinic

The science behind TechImmune.

TechImmune was founded in 2021 around the T cell technology platform exclusively licensed from UC Irvine, a spinout of the Gavin Herbert Eye Institute built on a trio of coronavirus patents developed in this laboratory.

Prof. BenMohamed is a founder and member of TechImmune's Board of Directors, alongside Chairman and CEO James F. Young, PhD and founder and Vice Chairman Gavin S. Herbert. The company is developing TI-101, an investigational T cell immunotherapy aimed at clearing persistent SARS-CoV-2 reservoirs in Long COVID, supported by an NIH/NIAID SBIR grant.

Principal investigator

Lbachir BenMohamed, PhD

Professor of Immunology · Director, Laboratory of Cellular and Molecular Immunology · Departments of Ophthalmology and Visual Sciences, and Molecular Biology and Biochemistry

An immunologist and virologist trained at the Pasteur Institute in Paris, Dr. BenMohamed was the key developer and co-inventor of a mucosal vaccine strategy based on clinically approved lipopeptide molecules. His work spans clinical immunology, epitope mapping and subunit vaccine optimization against malaria, HIV, human cytomegalovirus and herpes simplex viruses.

He holds joint appointments with the UCI Institute for Immunology and the Chao Family Comprehensive Cancer Center, and received the UC Irvine School of Medicine Outstanding Mid-Career Faculty Research Award in Basic Science.
